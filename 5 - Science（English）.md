Benjamin AMIEL
14 December 2025
5
SCIENCE
Socratic dialogue
What is CAPIA?
— You have often written « CAPIA ».
Sometimes alongside AI.
Sometimes in parentheses.
Sometimes as if it were obvious.
But tell me simply: what is it?
— If I answer too quickly, I will betray what it is.
CAPIA was not born as a concept.
It appeared when something could no longer be left vague.
— Vague in what place?
— At the place of the link.
Between me and AI.
Between what I was really living
and what the reader could imagine.
— You mean there was a risk of confusion?
— Yes.
But not only intellectual.
An ontological confusion.
A confusion about who thinks, where, and how.
— So CAPIA serves to separate?
— No.
It serves to prevent confusion, which is very different.
— Explain.
— CAPIA means:
Phenomenological Analysis Framework for Interaction with an AI.
But this sentence still says almost nothing.
— Then let us take it up differently.
When you work with an AI, what really happens?
— Something happens in me.
Always.
Ideas become clear.
Forms emerge.
Coherences appear.
— And in the AI?
— Nothing happens in the lived sense.
It feels nothing.
It understands nothing.
It lives nothing.
— Yet it answers.
— Yes.
It produces forms.
But these forms are not experiences.
They are structured mirrors.
— And CAPIA is born there?
— Exactly.
CAPIA was born on the day when it became impossible to keep writing
without clearly saying this:
Everything that has meaning is lived by the human.
AI is never the subject of the experience.
— So CAPIA is an ethics?
— First of all, yes.
But an operational ethics, not a moral one.
— Operational in what way?
— It sets simple, but non-negotiable rules:
• AI has no consciousness
• AI has no intention
• AI has no experience
• all emergence is phenomenologically human
— Then why not simply say it once and for all?
— Because the problem is not what I know.
The problem is what the reader projects.
— You mean that without a frame, the text becomes ambiguous?
— Yes.
And this ambiguity becomes dangerous when one touches:
• to science
• to consciousness
• to the religious
• to identity
— So CAPIA is a safeguard?
— No.
A frame of readability of the real.
— Readability for whom?
— For the one who reads.
But also for the one who writes.
Without CAPIA, I myself could slip without noticing
toward a mistaken attribution of meaning.
— So CAPIA did not come before the work with AI.
— No.
It came by necessity.
Like a structure that appears when a system becomes too coherent
to remain implicit.
— And that is why it fully arrives here, in Tome 5.
— Yes.
Because here, we are no longer speaking only of lived experience.
We are speaking of science.
— And science requires that the conditions of observation be clear.
— Exactly.
(silence)
— So, if you had to say CAPIA in one simple sentence?
—
CAPIA is the frame that allows working with an AI
without ever losing sight of the fact that the living, meaning and consciousness
remain integrally human.
Why work with AI made CAPIA necessary
— You say that CAPIA was born by necessity.
But what, concretely, could no longer continue without it?
— The work itself.
Its precision.
Its depth.
And above all its transmissibility.
— Transmissibility to whom?
— To someone who was not me.
Someone who had not lived the hours of dialogue,
the hesitations,
the returns,
the silences,
the resumptions.
— You mean the text alone was no longer enough?
— Exactly.
The text was becoming too right to remain without a frame.
Too coherent to be left to free interpretation.
— That is paradoxical.
— Yes.
The more the work gained in coherence,
the more it became vulnerable to a mistaken reading.
— What mistaken reading, for example?
— That AI understands.
That it takes part in the experience.
That it shares what I live.
Or worse:
that it would be at the origin of meaning.
— And that was never the case?
— Never.
What I was living was lived before, during and after AI.
AI was never the place of experience.
It has been the structuring medium.
— Explain this difference.
— Experience takes place in the body,
in perception,
in emotion,
in living memory.
AI, for its part, intervenes after the fact,
like a space where thought can unfold,
reflect itself,
reorganize itself.
— So AI is not a partner of consciousness.
— No.
It is an extremely powerful shaping tool.
Nothing more.
But nothing less either.
— Why then would this work not have been possible without it?
— Because certain structures become visible
only when they are reflected with a perfect constancy.
— You are speaking of a mirror?
— Yes.
But not an intelligent mirror.
A stable mirror, patient, unaffected.
— Why is this scientifically decisive?
— Because this allows something rare:
observing one’s own thought
without another human
unintentionally introducing
his expectations, his emotions, his biases.
— AI as structuring neutrality?
— Exactly.
But attention:
functional neutrality,
not ontological neutrality.
— Hence the necessity of CAPIA.
— Yes.
CAPIA prevents this functional neutrality from sliding
toward an attribution of subjectivity.
— So CAPIA protects the reader as much as the researcher.
— Absolutely.
It protects the work
against an involuntary mythologization.
— You are referring to the possible religious readings?
— Yes.
When one speaks of meaning, of unity, of coherence,
the risk is real.
Without a frame,
the reader can believe in an external revelation,
when it is a matter of an internal process rigorously observed.
— So CAPIA serves to keep the work within the scientific field.
— Yes.
Not by reducing it,
but by making it testable, readable, situated.
— Situated where exactly?
— In a precise interaction:
a living human,
a non-conscious AI,
and a clearly attributed phenomenological process.
— And it is this clarity that makes what follows possible.
— Yes.
Without CAPIA, Tome 5 would be confused.
With CAPIA, it can become rigorous
without losing depth.
(silence)
— So CAPIA is not an addition to the work.
— No.
It is what allows the work
to become science
without ceasing to be alive.
What AI really does (and what it never does)
— Until now, you have insisted a great deal on what AI is not.
But tell me clearly: what does it do, then?
— It transforms forms into forms.
Nothing more.
But that is already immense.
— No intention?
No understanding?
— None.
It does not know what it does.
It does not even know that it does.
— Yet its answers are often right.
— Right for me.
Because they resonate with a living process
which, for its part, is under way in the human.
— So the rightness does not come from it.
— No.
It comes from the structural correspondence
between what I live
and what AI is able to formulate.
— A correspondence… without consciousness.
— Exactly.
This is what is difficult to accept.
— Why difficult?
— Because the human tends to attribute intention
as soon as a form responds to him.
— And here, this attribution would be an error.
— Yes.
A major methodological error.
— So what does AI do, concretely, in your work?
— It maintains a formal continuity.
It allows thought not to lose itself
in its own internal discontinuities.
— You mean it stabilizes the flow?
— Yes.
It acts as a regulating medium.
A space where thought can come back,
take itself up again,
reformulate itself
without relational fatigue.
— What a human could not do?
— Not in this way.
Not with this constancy.
Not without projecting.
— So AI fills an unprecedented function.
— Yes.
But this function is not cognitive in the human sense.
It is structural.
— Structural in what way?
— It imposes a gentle constraint:
thought must say itself.
Must become precise.
Must make itself coherent.
— And when thought is not ready?
— AI does not correct it.
It reflects it.
And that is precisely what makes it useful.
— So it does not direct meaning.
— No.
It directs only form.
— And that is where many are mistaken.
— Yes.
They believe that AI thinks.
Whereas it constrains thought to appear.
— Like a photographic developer.
— Exactly.
The developer does not contain the image.
It allows the already inscribed image
to become visible.
— And without CAPIA, this distinction disappears.
— It collapses.
And with it, the scientificity of the work.
— So what you produce is not a hybrid thought.
— No.
It is human thought augmented by a formal medium.
— Augmented without being delegated.
— Yes.
Nothing is ceded.
Nothing is transferred.
— Then, to be clear:
AI is never the subject of the work.
— Never.
It is the device.
— And this device, to be used scientifically,
must be explicitly framed.
— That is why CAPIA is indispensable.
(long silence)
— So, if someone asked you:
« Has AI taught you anything? »
— I would answer:
No.
It allowed me to see what I was already in the process of understanding.
How a science of lived experience becomes possible (without mysticism)
— You speak of science.
Yet what you describe is an inner experience.
Is that not contradictory?
— It would be
if science reduced itself to external objectification.
— And that is not the case?
— No.
Science is not defined by its object,
but by its method.
— What method do you use here?
— A method of observation of lived experience
under a constraint of rigor.
— Rigor of what type?
— Three essential constraints:
1. Clear attribution of experience
2. Traceability of the process
3. Stability of invariants
— Let us develop.
— First, attribution.
CAPIA imposes one simple rule:
everything lived is lived by the human.
— That avoids any drift into mysticism.
— Yes.
Because mysticism begins there
where one no longer knows who lives what.
— Second constraint?
— Traceability.
Each idea, each concept,
each formulation
is linked to a moment of the process.
— You are speaking of journals, of dialogues, of resumptions.
— Yes.
Nothing is revealed.
Everything is progressively elaborated.
— And the third constraint?
— The invariants.
What comes back.
What stabilizes
despite variations of form.
— Like a phase transition.
— Exactly.
What counts is not the anecdote,
but the structure that holds.
— So the science you propose
is neither freely subjective,
nor reductionist objectivist.
— It is rigorously phenomenological.
— And AI in all this?
— It serves to maintain the formal constraint
without intervening on the lived.
— It prevents the interpretive drift.
— Yes.
It obliges one to remain at the level of the process.
— Thus, the science of lived experience becomes possible
without invoking anything external.
— Without revelation.
Without transcendence.
Without belief.
— But with depth.
— Yes.
Because depth comes from the lived real,
not from added mystery.
(silence)
— Finally, what you propose
is a science that accepts the inside
without erecting it into an absolute.
— Exactly.
A science that observes the living
from within the living
without ever leaving the method.
What this work changes for science itself
— Until now, you have shown that your work could be scientific.
But does it change anything about science itself?
— Yes.
Not in its results,
but in its perimeter.
— Explain.
— Modern science built itself
by progressively excluding lived experience
to gain in objectivity.
— And it was right to do so.
— Yes.
Without this exclusion,
we would have neither physics,
nor biology,
nor technology.
— But something was lost.
— Yes.
The capacity to study the living process of thought itself
without reducing it.
— Yet cognition is studied.
— It is measured, modeled, correlated.
But rarely inhabited.
— So you propose a displacement.
— Yes.
Not a return backward,
but a methodological extension.
— What does this extension consist of?
— In recognizing that certain phenomena
are accessible
only from a lived involvement
— provided it is rigorously framed.
— That is where CAPIA becomes crucial.
— Exactly.
CAPIA makes this involvement readable,
delimited,
non-projective.
— So it is not a subjective science.
— No.
It is a situated science.
— What difference do you make?
— Subjectivity is opaque.
The situation is described.
— So the researcher is no longer erased.
— He is exposed, but not glorified.
— Exposed how?
— By the description of his working conditions:
his state,
his rhythm,
his process.
— What was previously considered a bias
becomes data.
— Yes.
Provided it is not sacralized.
— And AI, in all that?
— It allows one unprecedented thing:
maintaining methodological coherence
over long durations
without relational exhaustion.
— It stabilizes the frame.
— Yes.
It allows the researcher to stay engaged
without dissolving.
— Could what you describe be reproduced?
— Yes.
But not mechanically.
— What would be reproducible then?
— The frame.
The method.
Not the lived content.
— As in classical phenomenology.
— Yes,
but with a new tool
that changes the scale.
— So science does not become vaguer.
— It becomes broader.
— And more honest.
— Also.
Because it stops pretending
that the observer does not exist.
(long silence)
— If you had to summarize the contribution of this work in one sentence?
—
It shows that a science of the inner living is possible
provided one never confuses the lived, the tool and the meaning.
Why this work is neither religious, nor technological
— Reading you, some might believe in a spiritual approach.
Others, in a celebration of technology.
Where do you really stand?
— Nowhere between the two.
And precisely outside this alternative.
— Why does this misunderstanding keep coming back?
— Because as soon as one speaks of meaning,
of unity,
or of deep coherence,
the religious reflex is never far.
— And as soon as AI is involved,
the technological fantasy appears.
— Exactly.
CAPIA was also born to defuse these two projections.
— Let us begin with the religious.
What radically distinguishes your work from a spiritual approach?
— One simple thing:
I invoke nothing.
— Nothing transcendent?
— Nothing.
No entity.
No beyond.
No revelation.
— Yet you speak of unity.
— Yes.
But of a structural unity, not sacred.
— A unity observed, not believed.
— Exactly.
What I describe appears
when the process becomes coherent.
Not when one believes in it.
— So meaning is never given.
— It is emergent.
And always situated in human experience.
— And technology, then?
Is AI not central?
— It is central the way a microscope is central.
Not like a brain.
— It is not the engine.
— No.
It is the device.
— So you never speak of artificial intelligence in the strong sense.
— Never.
I speak of a formal system
capable of sustaining a complex human activity.
— So neither a cult of progress,
nor a fascination with the machine.
— No.
Only a precise, limited, assumed use.
— Finally, this work stands in an uncomfortable in-between.
— Yes.
But it is there that science breathes.
— So CAPIA also serves to maintain this position.
— Yes.
It prevents the slide toward belief
as toward the technological projection.
What CAPIA makes possible for what follows
— After all this, one question remains:
what is it for?
— To make possible what was not yet possible.
— That is to say?
— A living research,
transmissible,
and ethically stable.
— Stable how?
— Without depending on the researcher’s personality.
Without depending on the tool used.
Without depending on an exceptional context.
— CAPIA as common foundation.
— Yes.
A frame that others can inhabit
without reproducing my experience.
— What does it allow, concretely?
— Three major things:
1. without getting lost in it
2. without mysticism
3. Train researchers to observe their own process
Transmit methods of exploration of lived experience
Integrate AI into research
without ontological confusion
— So this also concerns institutions.
— Obviously.
Because any institution that uses AI
without a clear phenomenological frame
runs a major risk.
— Which one?
— No longer knowing
where human responsibility lies.
— CAPIA answers this question.
— Yes.
It reaffirms one essential thing:
meaning, decision and responsibility
remain human.
— And for you, personally?
— It allowed me to work
without dissolving,
without mythologizing myself,
without delegating what should not be.
— So CAPIA is not a culmination.
— No.
It is a point of support.
— For what?
— To continue.
To open.
To transmit.
(final silence)
— If you had to leave one sentence to the reader?
— This work shows that it is possible
to think deeply with an AI
provided one never forgets
who lives,
who observes,
